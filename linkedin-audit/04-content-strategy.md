# 4. 90-Day Content Strategy

## The strategy in one line

**Be known as the merchandiser who really understands cost.** Costing is your deepest skill. Very few people post about it well, and both recruiters and brand sourcing teams respect it. Everything else (production, communication, career, sourcing trends) supports that core.

## Content pillars and mix

| Pillar | Share | Why |
|--------|------:|-----|
| 1. Costing & consumption (your signature) | 35% | Builds authority. People save and share it. |
| 2. Production problems → solutions | 20% | Shows real-world experience |
| 3. Buyer & factory communication | 15% | Shows commercial maturity |
| 4. Industry trends & global sourcing (EU regulations, US tariffs, BD vs. competitors) | 15% | Attracts brand-side and international people |
| 5. Data & automation for merchandisers (your CSE edge) | 10% | Makes you different from every other merchandiser |
| 6. Career & leadership | 5% | Builds connection; keep it practical, not motivational |

## Posting rules

- **3 posts a week:** Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday at around **9:00–10:00 AM Bangladesh time**. That reaches Bangladesh in the morning and catches EU/Nordic readers early in their day (about 4–5 AM CET is too early, so also test **2:00–3:00 PM BDT**, which is 9–10 AM CET). Track which slot works better in the tracker.
- **Formats:** text-only posts with a strong first line, carousels (PDF documents) for costing breakdowns, and an occasional single image (a clear photo of a fabric, a marker or a trims card, with buyer branding removed).
- **First line:** must make someone stop scrolling. Under 12 words. No "I am happy to share."
- **Length:** 120–250 words for text posts. Use short paragraphs and white space.
- **Hashtags:** 3 at most, at the end. They barely affect reach now, but they help with categorisation. Rotate: #ApparelSourcing #GarmentCosting #Merchandising #Bangladesh #FashionSupplyChain #Denim #Knitwear
- **Links:** put external links in the first comment, not the post body.
- **Engagement:** reply to every comment within the first 2 hours. Spend 15 minutes before and after posting commenting on others' posts.
- **Confidentiality:** never post real buyer prices, factory names in a negative context, unreleased designs, or anything that identifies your employer's clients' internal data. Use "a Nordic brand" or "a US mass-market buyer", and use illustrative numbers.

### Post #2: Hidden cost drivers

> Your FOB isn't wrong because of the fabric price. It's wrong because of what you didn't count.
>
> 7 hidden cost drivers I check on every cost sheet:
>
> 1. **Shrinkage allowance.** A denim style washed to 8% shrinkage needs more fabric than the pattern suggests.
> 2. **Marker efficiency.** Quoting at 85% when the real marker is 80% is a silent loss on every piece.
> 3. **Size ratio.** Consumption at size M isn't the average if the ratio is heavy on L/XL.
> 4. **Minimums.** A trim MOQ of 5,000 on a 1,800-piece order means you pay for 3,200 you'll never use.
> 5. **Wash/print rejection.** If the wash rejection rate is 3%, that's 3% more fabric, CM and trims.
> 6. **Testing and inspection.** Third-party tests per colourway add up fast on multi-colour programs.
> 7. **Efficiency on small orders.** A line never reaches its target efficiency on a 1,200-piece order. Your CM must reflect that.
>
> None of these are visible on a buyer's target price sheet. All of them hit the margin.
>
> Which one do you see missed most often?
>
> #GarmentCosting #ApparelSourcing #Merchandising

### Post #3: Knit consumption

> How I calculate knit fabric consumption for a basic T-shirt, in 4 steps.
>
> *(Illustrative numbers; your spec sheet will differ.)*
>
> **1. Body:** (Body length + hem + shoulder allowance) × (½ chest + side seam allowance) × 2 (front + back)
> Example: (72 + 4) cm × (52 + 4) cm × 2 = 8,512 cm²
>
> **2. Sleeve:** (Sleeve length + allowances) × (sleeve width + allowance) × 2
> Example: (22 + 4) × (22 + 3) × 2 = 1,300 cm²
>
> **3. Convert to weight:** total area × GSM ÷ 10,000,000 = kg per piece
> (9,812 cm² × 160 GSM) ÷ 10,000,000 = 0.157 kg
>
> **4. Add wastage:** cutting + process loss, e.g. 10–12% for a basic tee
> 0.157 × 1.12 ≈ 0.176 kg per piece ≈ **2.11 kg per dozen**
>
> Then check it against the actual marker. If the marker says 2.25 kg/dz, the marker wins; your formula was the estimate.
>
> The mistakes I see most: forgetting the rib, using finished GSM instead of the GSM the fabric is bought at, and applying one wastage % to every style.
>
> Save this for your next costing.
>
> #GarmentCosting #Knitwear #Merchandising

### Post #4: Delayed shipment (structure post)

> A shipment is never late on the shipment date. It becomes late weeks earlier.
>
> Here's a pattern I've seen more than once, in a format I use for every production problem:
>
> **Issue:** Bulk fabric arrived 10 days late. Ex-factory date at risk.
> **Root cause:** The lab dip approval took 3 rounds, but nobody moved the fabric booking date in the T&A.
> **Impact:** Possible air shipment, or a discount request from the buyer.
> **Immediate action:** Split the cutting plan, prioritise the colours that had arrived, and add a second line for 6 days.
> **Corrective action:** Agree a partial shipment with the buyer *before* the delay became official.
> **Preventive action:** Every approval stage in the T&A now has a "downstream date" linked to it. If the approval slips, the fabric date turns red automatically.
>
> The lesson: tell the buyer early, bring a plan, and fix the system, not just the order.
>
> What's your earliest warning sign that a shipment is in danger?
>

### Post #5: CM costing

> CM isn't a number you negotiate. It's a number you calculate, and *then* negotiate.
>
> The basic logic:
>
> **CM per piece = (SMV ÷ line efficiency) × cost per minute (CPM)**
>
> *(Illustrative example:)*
> • SMV: 8.0 minutes (basic tee)
> • Line efficiency: 60%
> • Factory CPM: USD 0.06 (labour + overhead + margin per available minute)
>
> (8.0 ÷ 0.60) × 0.06 = **USD 0.80 per piece, or about USD 9.60/dozen**
>
> Now look at what moves it:
> • Efficiency goes from 60% to 70% → CM drops to USD 0.69
> • The buyer adds a chest pocket (+1.2 SMV) → CM rises to USD 0.92
> • Order quantity is small → efficiency never reaches 60%
>
> So when a buyer says "CM is too high," the real conversation is about the SMV, the expected efficiency and the order size, not just the final number.
>
> Factories that can explain their CM this way win more negotiations.
>
> #GarmentCosting #CMCost #ApparelManufacturing

### Post #6: Target price gap

> The buyer's target is 15% below your cost. What do you do?
>
> Here's the order I work in:
>
> **1. Check your own sheet first.** Is the consumption from the real marker? Is the wastage realistic or padded?
>
> **2. Break the gap down.** A 15% gap is never one thing. Maybe it's 6% fabric, 4% wash, 3% trims, 2% CM.
>
> **3. Offer options, not refusals.**
> • Fabric: 180 → 170 GSM, or a different mill with equivalent quality
> • Wash: reduce steps, for example one enzyme wash instead of enzyme + bleach
> • Trims: a standard button instead of a custom one, a shared label
> • Order: a larger quantity, or combining colours to improve efficiency
>
> **4. Show the trade-off.** "We can reach X if we change Y. Here's the impact on look and quality."
>
> **5. Know your walk-away number.** Some orders are more expensive to take than to lose.
>
> Buyers don't respect a quick yes. They respect a clear cost logic.
>
> #CostNegotiation #ApparelSourcing #Merchandising

### Post #7: Denim costing

> In denim, the fabric is visible. The wash is where cost hides.
>
> When I cost a 5-pocket jean, these are the lines that move the price most after fabric:
>
> • **Wash recipe:** every step (desize, stone, enzyme, bleach, tint, softener) adds chemical, water and time
> • **Dry process:** whiskers, hand-sanding, grinding, PP spray, all manual work charged per piece
> • **Shrinkage:** fabric with 2% warp shrinkage vs 6% changes the consumption and the pattern
> • **Rejection rate:** heavy washes carry a higher shade and damage rejection
> • **Lead time:** wash development rounds take longer than sewing
>
> A tip that has saved arguments: get the wash cost broken down by step from the laundry, not as one lump sum. That's where you find room to negotiate.
>
> Denim people, what's the most expensive wash effect you've had to cost?
>
> #Denim #GarmentCosting #ApparelSourcing

### Post #8: Career lessons

> From Assistant Merchandiser in 2015 to Senior Merchandiser today. 5 things I wish I'd known on day one:
>
> 1. **Learn costing early.** The person who understands the cost sheet gets a seat in the important meetings.
> 2. **Write emails people can act on.** Short, numbered, with a deadline. Long emails get skipped.
> 3. **Your T&A is your shield.** When something goes wrong, it shows what happened and when.
> 4. **Factories remember how you treated them in a crisis.** Respect builds speed.
> 5. **Learn one skill outside merchandising.** For me it was data and Excel. It made every other skill faster.
>
> To juniors starting out: the first 2 years feel like follow-up work. They're actually where you learn how everything connects.

### Post #9: Fabric price analysis

> "The fabric price went up 8%." OK, but why?
>
> Before accepting a fabric price increase, I break the quote into its parts:
>
> • **Yarn:** has the cotton or polyester yarn price actually moved? Check the market trend for the same count.
> • **Knitting/weaving charge:** is the rate per kg or per metre the same as last season?
> • **Dyeing/finishing:** has the shade changed (dark and special colours cost more)? Is there a new finish?
> • **Certification premium:** organic, recycled or BCI content adds a premium. Is it the same %?
> • **Quantity:** a smaller order might have moved the mill into a higher price slab.
> • **Currency:** is the quote in BDT converted at a different rate?
>
> Half the time, the increase is real. The other half, one part changed and the rest came along for the ride.
>
> Ask for the breakdown. It's the single best habit in fabric negotiation.
>
> #FabricSourcing #GarmentCosting #ApparelSourcing
